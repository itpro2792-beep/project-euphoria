Hard boundary

When this page is not the tool

Household protocols stop when someone may be in immediate danger, cannot safely say no or leave, asks for help staying safe, or when abuse, threats, force, or coercion are present. A person does not need perfect words before concern becomes real.

If you are in the United States or its territories, call or text 988 for crisis support. In a life-threatening emergency, call 911. Elsewhere, use your local emergency or crisis service. Reaching outside the household never violates a protocol.

    Seven household practices

    The Protocols

    Each card now states consent, exact words, a stop condition, and when not to use it. These are practices we developed and use—not proof that they will work for everyone.

    The Talking Sticktake turns without fighting for the floor

    May help when: two or more people interrupt each other, care about the conversation, and can freely agree to take turns.

    Use only if: everyone may decline, request a break, or leave without punishment. Choose the object and a time limit together while calm.

    Start with: “Can we use this object for three-minute turns? Either of us can pause the conversation.”

    1. The holder gets the floor for the agreed time.
    2. Anyone may request the object; nobody grabs it.
    3. When time ends, the holder finishes one sentence and passes it.
    4. The object manages the floor, not truth, decisions, or power.

    Stop and repair: if someone monopolizes, mocks, grabs, or cannot safely wait, pause. Later, shorten the turns or retire the tool.

    Do not use when: someone is afraid to say no, leaving is unsafe, or the object becomes a way to control, silence, or delay urgent help.

    The Safe Worda voluntary brake for a monologue

    May help when: a talker wants help noticing when they have passed the listener’s capacity.

    Use only if: the talker proposes the word, everyone agrees to its meaning, and anyone may revise or end the agreement later.

    Start with: “My word is ____. If you say it, I will stop, take five quiet seconds, and ask what you need.”

    1. Say the word once, plainly—not as ridicule.
    2. The talker stops without argument or penalty.
    3. Take the agreed pause, then choose whether to continue later.
    4. Review misuse only after the moment is calm.

    Stop and repair: retaliation, mockery, or repeated weaponization ends the protocol until a new voluntary agreement exists.

    Do not use when: it would prevent someone from naming danger, leaving, seeking help, or responding to an urgent need.

    “Off the Record”agree on privacy before disclosure

    May help when: people who write, record, or share notes want a conversation that will not be documented or quoted later.

    Use only if: the listener agrees before the disclosure and states any real limits to confidentiality in advance. No surprise rules.

    Start with: “I want this off the record. Can you agree not to record or repeat it, within the safety and legal limits we already named?”

    1. Wait for an explicit yes before continuing.
    2. Do not record, summarize, quote, or weaponize the protected material.
    3. Either person may stop before more is said.
    4. The phrase cannot retroactively erase promises, actions, or accountability.

    Stop and repair: if the limits were unclear, stop the disclosure and clarify them. If trust is broken, do not reuse the protocol until repaired.

    Do not use to: conceal abuse, threats, imminent harm, coercion, or information someone is legally or ethically required to report. Never promise secrecy you cannot keep.

    The Loop Detectorflag repetition without diagnosing the person

    May help when: someone notices that their thinking or conversation repeats and explicitly wants an outside signal.

    Use only if: the person receiving the signal chooses the detector and the word. Either may end the role at any time.

    Start with: “If you hear me repeat the same ground, please say ‘Loop’ once. I can answer ‘not a loop,’ and we drop it.”

    1. Say the word once, kindly and without a lecture.
    2. The receiver chooses what to do next.
    3. If the receiver says “not a loop,” the detector stops arguing.
    4. Review false positives later; they can still hurt.

    Stop and repair: if the signal raises tension or feels dismissive, suspend it and revisit consent when calm.

    Do not use to: dismiss grief, disagreement, trauma, a new fact, or an inconvenient truth; label someone; win an argument; or override a request to stop.

    No-Flinchheavy is not automatically emergency

    May help when: someone needs to say something heavy and wants calm listening instead of panic, fixing, or an automatic script.

    Use only if: the speaker can choose what kind of response they want and the listener remains free to ask a direct safety question when there is genuine concern.

    Start with: “This is heavy, but I am not in immediate danger. I need five uninterrupted minutes and listening, not fixing.”

    1. Listen without punishment, spectacle, or using the disclosure later as ammunition.
    2. Ask what is wanted: listening, reflection, planning, company, or outside help.
    3. If safety is unclear, ask once and plainly: “Are you safe right now?” Asking directly does not violate the protocol.
    4. If the answer is no, unclear, or behavior suggests immediate risk, shift from this protocol to real-time support.

    Stop and repair: if the listener becomes overwhelmed, say so without blaming the speaker and help identify another safe listener or support.

    Do not use to: forbid safety questions, block outside help, demand secrecy, minimize warning signs, or require a person in danger to phrase the situation perfectly.

    Paved Crossingsmake a voluntary plan before a known risk

    May help when: a person recognizes a recurring risky moment and wants to design their own tripwires, contacts, transportation, and exit plan in advance.

    Use only if: the person most affected co-designs the plan while calm and able to consent. Every helper knows exactly what they may and may not do.

    Start with: “If ____ happens, I want you to say ____. I will ____. If that fails, contact ____. I can pause or revoke this plan at any time. This plan never authorizes force or delayed care.”

    1. Name observable tripwires, not character judgments.
    2. Name a willing backstop and a lawful, noncoercive action.
    3. Write the exit, ride, call, or support plan in plain words.
    4. Review it after use, including whether it raised or lowered risk.

    Stop and repair: if the plan becomes controlling, secret, unsafe, or impossible to follow, retire it and seek appropriate outside guidance.

    Do not use when: the plan requires control or cannot be freely revoked. It never authorizes force, restraint, confinement, surveillance, confiscation, forced transport, retaliation, blocked communication, or delayed emergency care. It is not a substitute for a clinician-created safety plan or professional harm-reduction guidance.

    Receiptsdate what worked, failed, and remains unknown

    May help when: big claims or despair make it hard to see what actually happened.

    Use only if: the ledger belongs to the person writing it. Nobody may require another person to log, disclose, defend, or surrender entries.

    Start with: “Today, this worked: ____. This did not: ____. This remains pending: ____.”

    1. Date every entry using the writer’s real local date.
    2. Keep positive, negative, and pending evidence.
    3. Ask “what, when, and how much?” without turning the ledger into a trial.
    4. Change a pending claim only when new evidence arrives.

    Stop and repair: if logging becomes compulsive, punitive, performative, or unsafe, reduce the frequency, change the labels, or stop.

    Do not use to: surveil another person, manufacture proof, erase context, replace professional records, or call one household’s experience universal evidence.

    Your control

    Back up or erase your local data

    Clearing browser data, using a private window, changing browser profiles, or losing this device can erase local entries. A downloaded backup is the only portable copy this page creates.

    What “local” means in this version

    The page has no analytics, account, form submission, advertising code, or third-party script. Its browser policy blocks fetch, XHR, WebSocket, EventSource, and beacon-style connections from this page’s JavaScript; that policy does not make the browser, extensions, clipboard, downloads, or shared-origin storage private.

    Entries are stored with localStorage for the shared https://itpro2792-beep.github.io origin. Browser storage is scoped to that entire origin, not to /project-euphoria/toolbox/; code on another path under the same origin can potentially read it. A dedicated origin with reviewed isolation is required before this can honestly be called private-by-design. Clipboard contents may also be visible to your operating system, browser, or synced devices after you copy them.

    To prevent two tabs from writing at the same time, the page requires an exclusive browser Web Lock for persistence. A second Toolbox tab can read, copy, and export but cannot save; close the writer tab and reload the other tab to transfer control. If the browser does not support Web Locks, persistence stays disabled instead of risking a last-writer-wins overwrite.

    These practices grew from lived use in one household. That is experience, not validation. We have not established effectiveness rates, failure rates, safe-use populations, or clinical outcomes. The public alpha exists so the instructions, limits, and failures can become more honest—not so early experience can masquerade as proof.
